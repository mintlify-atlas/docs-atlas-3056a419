87% of employers expect candidates to negotiate, yet most people accept the first offer. That gap costs the average professional hundreds of thousands of dollars over a career — conservative estimates put the lifetime cost of never negotiating at over $500,000 in foregone compensation. The `salary-negotiation-prep` skill researches market rates for your specific role, level, location, and industry; calculates your true total compensation picture; builds your negotiation position from the ground up; and gives you word-for-word scripts for every scenario — counter-offers, competing offers, fixed-base situations, and benefits conversations — so you can negotiate confidently and professionally without guessing what to say.

## What Negotiation Prep Covers

This skill builds you a complete negotiation strategy, not just a number to throw out:

- **Market rate research** — pulls compensation benchmarks segmented by role, level, location, industry, and company size to give you a defensible range
- **Total compensation breakdown** — converts the full package into apples-to-apples annual numbers: base, equity (RSUs vs. options), target bonus, signing bonus, benefits, and perks
- **Your BATNA** — identifies your Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement so you know exactly what your walk-away point is and how much leverage you actually have
- **Anchor point strategy** — explains how and when to name a number first, and what number to anchor on to pull the final figure in your direction
- **Counter-offer script generation** — produces phone and email scripts tailored to your specific offer, role, and experience level
- **Competing offer leverage scripts** — how to use other offers ethically and effectively without burning the relationship
- **Benefits negotiation tactics** — a complete playbook for when the hiring manager tells you the base salary cannot move

<Note>
  Studies show that 87% of hiring managers have flexibility on the initial offer. The first offer is almost never the final offer — it is a starting position, not a statement of your worth. Companies routinely budget a negotiation buffer of 5–15% above the first number they present, expecting candidates to push back.
</Note>

## Market Rate Research Sources

Accurate negotiation starts with accurate data. The skill draws on multiple sources and shows you how to triangulate across them, because each source has its own biases and gaps:

**For tech roles:**
- **Levels.fyi** — the most reliable source for total compensation at tech companies, broken down by level, team, and location. Updated continuously with user-submitted offers.
- **Blind** — anonymous forum posts from employees at specific companies; useful for reading the room on what current employees actually make and whether equity is ever refreshed.

**For general roles:**
- **LinkedIn Salary** — broad coverage across industries; more reliable for non-tech roles than Levels.fyi.
- **Glassdoor** — useful as a directional reference, but submissions skew toward unhappy employees sharing low numbers and happy employees sharing high numbers. Use it for calibration, not as a single source of truth.
- **PayScale and Salary.com** — good for non-tech professional roles where Levels data doesn't exist.

**For government-required disclosures:**
- **H-1B salary data** (publicly available via the Department of Labor) — every company that sponsors H-1B visas is legally required to disclose the prevailing wage. This is actual offered compensation data, not self-reported. Highly reliable for companies that hire internationally.
- **Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment Statistics** — the most comprehensive public dataset for compensation across all industries. Less granular than commercial sources but free from submission bias.

**How to triangulate:** The skill builds your negotiation range by pulling data from at least three sources, finding the median and 75th percentile across all of them, and adjusting for your specific situation (years of experience, specialized skills, current market demand, competing offers). One source is a data point; three sources are a range you can defend.

## Negotiation Strategy Framework

<Steps>
  <Step title="Anchor high — your first counter should be 10–20% above your target">
    Anchoring is one of the most well-documented effects in negotiation research: the first number stated disproportionately influences where the conversation lands. If the company offers $140,000 and your target is $155,000, your counter should be $165,000–$170,000 — not $155,000. This gives you room to land at your actual target while appearing to move toward them. Anchoring too close to your target leaves nothing to give.

    Exceptions: If your market research shows the offer is already above the 75th percentile, anchoring too high can damage your credibility. The skill flags this and adjusts the recommended counter accordingly.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Focus on total comp — salary, equity, and signing bonus are all negotiable">
    Base salary is just one lever. Companies often have more flexibility on equity grants, signing bonuses, and accelerated vesting than on base salary, because base affects payroll permanently while a signing bonus is a one-time line item. If you're a public company hire, a higher RSU grant may be worth more than the equivalent base increase over a four-year period.

    Before negotiating, the skill builds a total compensation table for your offer so you know what you're actually being offered — not just the number in the subject line.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Use competing offers as leverage — even if you prefer this role">
    A real competing offer is your strongest negotiating asset. It proves market validation of your value in a way that no amount of salary research can replicate. The key is to frame it as information, not a threat: "I want to be transparent — I have another offer at $X. This role is my first choice, but I want to make sure we can get to a number that makes sense."

    The skill generates competing-offer scripts that are honest, specific, and framed in a way that keeps the relationship warm rather than turning the conversation adversarial.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Get everything in writing before accepting verbally">
    Verbal promises about raises, promotions, remote flexibility, and bonus targets are unenforceable. This is not cynicism — it's good practice. Hiring managers move on, companies reorganize, and the person who promised you a mid-year review will be gone in 14 months. The skill reminds you to request an updated offer letter that reflects every term agreed upon in the negotiation before you give verbal acceptance.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Negotiate benefits when base is 'fixed' — there is almost always somewhere to move">
    When a recruiter says "the salary band is firm," the conversation isn't over. Signing bonuses, additional equity, accelerated vesting, additional PTO, remote flexibility, professional development budget, and earlier performance review cycles are all negotiable — often with a different budget owner than the person controlling base salary. The skill maps out each alternative lever and provides scripts for each one.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Sample Counter-Offer Script

The skill generates scripts tailored to your specific offer, but here is the core structure it uses for a phone or video negotiation:

```text
"Thank you so much for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role 
and the team. After researching market rates for [Role] in [Location] and 
considering my [X] years of [specific relevant experience], I was hoping we 
could get to [target number]. Is there flexibility there?"
```

This structure works because it leads with enthusiasm (protecting the relationship), anchors your ask in research and evidence (making it defensible rather than arbitrary), names a specific number (vague asks get vague responses), and ends with an open question (inviting dialogue rather than forcing a yes/no).

**Email version for following up:**

```text
Subject: [Your Name] — Offer Discussion

Hi [Name],

Thank you again for the offer to join [Company] as [Title]. I'm really 
excited about the opportunity and the team — especially [something specific 
from your conversations].

I've had a chance to review the details and wanted to discuss compensation. 
Based on market data for [Role] in [Location] at this experience level, and 
reflecting on the scope of the role, I was hoping we could get to a base 
salary of [target number] rather than [offered number].

I'm also flexible on structure — open to discussing signing bonus or equity 
if that's easier to work with. Would you have time for a quick call to discuss?

Thank you again for this opportunity. I'm looking forward to finding something 
that works for both of us.

Best,
[Your Name]
```

<Warning>
  Never negotiate over email if you can help it. Negotiate by phone or video — tone and relationship matter enormously, and it is far easier to misread a counter-offer in writing as aggressive or ungrateful than it is in conversation. Use email to confirm what was agreed verbally, and to provide a paper trail, but have the actual negotiation conversation live.
</Warning>

## Total Compensation Calculator

Before you negotiate, you need to know what you're actually comparing. The skill builds this breakdown for every offer:

| Component | How to Value It |
|---|---|
| Base salary | Annual, as stated |
| Target bonus | Base × bonus % (note: this is a *target*, not a guarantee — ask about historical payout rates) |
| Signing bonus | One-time; mentally discount it — it doesn't recur |
| Equity (RSUs) | Total grant ÷ vesting period in years = annual value at current share price; adjust for vesting cliff |
| Equity (options) | (Current price − strike price) × number of options; higher risk, potentially higher upside |
| 401(k) match | Base × match % up to cap; 100% return on that portion of your salary |
| Benefits | Health insurance, dental, vision — use $15,000–$25,000/year as a rough proxy for premium employer plans vs. bare-minimum coverage |

**Example total comp calculation:**

```text
Base Salary:              $155,000
Target Bonus (15%):        $23,250
RSU Grant (4-year):       $200,000 → $50,000/year at current price
401(k) Match (4%):          $6,200
Benefits (premium plan):   ~$18,000 estimated value

Total Annual Comp:         $252,450
```

Two offers with $10,000 difference in base salary may have $40,000 difference in total annual compensation once equity and benefits are included. Always compare total packages.

## When the Base Is "Fixed"

When a recruiter tells you the base salary cannot move — sometimes genuinely, sometimes as a negotiating position — there are still meaningful levers to pull. The skill generates specific scripts for each:

**Signing bonus:** The most common alternative. Signing bonuses come from a different budget than payroll and are a one-time cost, making them easier to approve. "If the base is firm, could we discuss a signing bonus of $X to help bridge the gap between the offer and where I was expecting to land?" Even $10,000–$20,000 in signing bonus meaningfully changes your year-one total compensation.

**Equity grant size:** Request a larger RSU grant or additional stock options. For companies with strong equity programs, this can be worth significantly more than an equivalent base increase over the vesting period. "Could we look at increasing the equity grant to offset the base? I'm comfortable with the vesting schedule."

**Accelerated vesting:** If they won't increase the grant size, ask for an accelerated vesting cliff — going from a 12-month cliff to a 6-month cliff means you reach liquidity faster. This doesn't cost the company more in total but meaningfully changes your risk exposure.

**Title or level:** Getting leveled up (e.g., from Senior to Staff, or from Manager to Senior Manager) changes the salary band you're in — which often unlocks higher base automatically. It also affects your trajectory for future raises and promotions.

**Remote flexibility:** An additional day of remote work per week is worth thousands of dollars annually in commute savings, time recovered, and quality of life. If commuting costs are meaningful, put a real number on this.

**Additional PTO:** One extra week of PTO is roughly 2% of your base salary in effective compensation. For a $150,000 salary, that's $3,000 in value.

**Earlier performance review cycle:** Request a 6-month performance review with compensation adjustment tied to it, rather than waiting 12 months. Get the language in writing: "Performance review at 6 months, with base salary adjustment to [target number] upon positive review."

## Negotiation Scenarios

<Accordion title="Scenario: They ask for your salary expectations first">
  Giving a number first before you have an offer means you might anchor yourself too low — or too high, which can screen you out of the process early. The goal is to get them to name a range first.

  **Deflection script:**
  ```text
  "I'm still learning about the full scope of the role, so I want to 
  make sure I'm giving you a number that makes sense. What's the range 
  you've budgeted for this position?"
  ```

  If they push back and insist:
  ```text
  "Based on my research for this role and market, I'm targeting $X–$Y, 
  but I'm flexible and more focused on finding the right fit overall."
  ```

  Note: Several states (California, New York, Colorado, Washington) have salary transparency laws requiring employers to disclose ranges. Know whether the law applies in your location.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Scenario: You have a competing offer">
  A competing offer is leverage only if you're willing to use it truthfully. Never fabricate or exaggerate an offer — recruiters talk to each other, and it destroys trust if discovered.

  **Script:**
  ```text
  "I want to be transparent with you — I have another offer at $X from 
  [Company or 'a company in the same space']. This role is genuinely my 
  first choice because [specific reason: the mission, the team, the 
  problem you're solving], but I want to see if we can close the gap 
  on compensation so I can make a clean decision. Is there any flexibility 
  to get closer to $Y?"
  ```

  Framing it as "my first choice" removes the ultimatum dynamic and makes it easier for the recruiter to advocate internally for a better number.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Scenario: They come back with a number that's still too low">
  One round of negotiation often isn't the end. If their revised offer is better but still short of your target, you can push once more — but the second counter should be smaller in magnitude than the first to signal you're moving toward closure, not digging in further.

  **Script:**
  ```text
  "I appreciate you going back to the team on this — that means a lot. 
  The revised offer is closer, but I was really hoping to get to $X. 
  Is there any room left, even if it's a one-time signing bonus to close 
  the gap? I'm ready to sign as soon as we can land there."
  ```

  Tying "ready to sign" to your ask gives them a clear call to action and signals that you're not going to keep negotiating indefinitely.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Scenario: They ask about your current salary">
  In many U.S. states (California, New York, Illinois, Massachusetts, and others), asking about current salary history is illegal. Know your jurisdiction.

  Even where it's legal, you don't have to answer directly. Current salary anchors the negotiation to your past rather than to your market value.

  **Redirect script:**
  ```text
  "My current compensation structure is pretty different from this role, 
  so I don't think it's a useful comparison. What I can tell you is that 
  based on my research for this level and market, I'm targeting $X–$Y."
  ```
</Accordion>

## Best Practices

- **Always negotiate.** The risk of losing the offer by negotiating professionally is close to zero — companies don't rescind offers because a candidate asked for more. The only way you can lose by negotiating is if you do it rudely or with fabricated information.

- **Express genuine enthusiasm every time you push back.** Every counter should start from a place of real interest in the role. Negotiation that feels adversarial tends to conclude faster, and not in your favor. Negotiation that feels collaborative tends to get the other party invested in finding a solution.

- **Prepare your walk-away number before the conversation starts.** Know the minimum package you will accept. This prevents you from agreeing to something in the moment that you'll resent later.

- **Don't accept on the spot, even if the offer is great.** Take 24 hours to review any offer before accepting, even if you're planning to say yes. It sets a professional tone, and in those 24 hours, you might think of something else worth asking about.

- **Get the final agreement in writing before you resign from your current role.** Verbal acceptances are not binding. Request an updated offer letter reflecting all negotiated terms, review it carefully, and only then give notice at your current job.
