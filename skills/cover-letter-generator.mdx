Most cover letters are generic and ignored. Hiring managers can tell within the first two sentences whether you've read the job description or simply swapped the company name into a template. This skill builds a compelling, specific cover letter by mining your resume for the most relevant experience and directly mirroring the job description's language — so every paragraph answers the unspoken question: *why you, why this company, why now*.

## Cover Letter Structure

A strong cover letter follows a three-part structure that respects the reader's time while making a persuasive case for your candidacy. Each section has a distinct job to do.

**1. Opening Hook**

The opening must earn the reader's attention in the first two sentences. Avoid "I am excited to apply for…" — it signals a template. Instead, open with a specific reason why this company or this role, grounded in something real: a recent company announcement, a product you use, a problem you recognize from your own work history.

**2. Body (2–3 Paragraphs)**

Each body paragraph maps one of your strongest, most relevant achievements directly to one of the role's key requirements. Pull language from the job description itself — if the posting says "cross-functional stakeholder management," use that phrase when describing your experience. The goal is to make the reader feel like your background was written for this posting.

**3. Close**

End with a clear next-step ask, not a passive "I look forward to hearing from you." Reference something specific about the role or team, express genuine enthusiasm, and include your preferred contact information so the reader doesn't have to search for it.

## What Inputs Are Needed

The skill works best when you provide all of the following:

- **Your resume** — used to extract the most relevant achievements, titles, companies, and metrics
- **The full job description** — the single most important input; every paragraph of the letter should trace back to specific language in the posting
- **Hiring manager's name** *(optional)* — enables a proper salutation ("Dear Sarah Chen,") instead of the generic "Dear Hiring Manager,"
- **A specific reason you're interested in this company** *(optional)* — recent funding rounds, a product launch, a piece of press, or a mission statement you genuinely connect with; this elevates the opening hook from plausible to compelling

If you don't have the hiring manager's name, the skill will recommend the best fallback salutation ("Dear [Department] Team," tends to feel warmer than "To Whom It May Concern").

## Customization Options

The skill supports several customization dimensions before or after the first draft:

**Tone**
- *Formal* — appropriate for finance, law, enterprise sales, and government roles; no contractions, full titles, measured language
- *Conversational* — appropriate for startups, creative agencies, and consumer tech; warmer voice, first-person energy, personality visible

**Length**
- *3 paragraphs* — hook, single body paragraph, close; best for roles where cover letters are skimmed
- *4 paragraphs* — hook, two body paragraphs, close; gives room to address a second key qualification or handle a potential concern (career gap, industry change, overqualification)

**Emphasis**
- *Technical skills* — leads with tools, platforms, and systems; ideal for engineering, data, and DevOps roles
- *Leadership and management* — leads with team impact, hiring, org design, and strategic ownership; ideal for director-level and above
- *Culture fit* — emphasizes mission alignment, values, and why this specific company appeals to you; ideal for mission-driven organizations and smaller teams

## Opening Hook: Before and After

The difference between a forgettable application and one that gets a response often comes down to the first sentence. Here is the same candidate applying to the same role, written two ways:

**Generic opening:**
> "I am excited to apply for the Senior Engineer position at Acme Corp. I have five years of experience in distributed systems and believe I would be a great fit for your team."

**Strong opening:**
> "When I saw Acme Corp's $50M Series B last month, I immediately recognized the exact scaling challenge you'd face — I spent the last three years solving that same problem at Stripe, taking our payments infrastructure from 10,000 to 10 million daily transactions without a reliability regression."

The strong version demonstrates company-specific knowledge, names a directly relevant achievement, and makes the reader want to know more — all in two sentences.

## Full Cover Letter Workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Input Parsing">
    The skill reads your resume and the job description side by side. It identifies the role's top three to five requirements (the skills or competencies that appear most frequently or most prominently in the posting) and cross-references them against your experience to find the strongest matches.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Opening Hook Generation">
    Using the company name, role context, and any additional interest you've noted, the skill drafts an opening hook that opens with something specific — not a statement of intent, but a moment of recognition or insight that shows you've done your homework.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Body Paragraph Drafting">
    Each body paragraph follows the formula: **[Their Requirement] + [Your Exact Experience] + [Measurable Result]**. The skill mirrors the job description's vocabulary, integrating keywords naturally so the letter reads well to both humans and any ATS scanning supporting documents.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Gap Handling (If Needed)">
    If your resume doesn't cover one of the role's stated requirements, the skill identifies the gap and drafts language that addresses it proactively — either by pointing to a transferable skill, a self-directed learning effort, or a complementary strength that compensates.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Close and Call to Action">
    The close expresses specific enthusiasm (not generic excitement), names a concrete contribution you'd make in the role, and includes a clear ask — an interview, a conversation, or a follow-up — along with your contact details.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Quality Check and Alternatives">
    The skill runs a checklist against 10 quality criteria before delivering the final letter: no template opener, specific company knowledge present, at least one metric included, no typos, appropriate length, confident tone. It also generates two alternative opening hooks so you can choose the angle that feels most authentic to you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Handling Common Scenarios

**No hiring manager name:** The skill recommends "Dear [Team Name] Team," (e.g., "Dear Data Engineering Team,") over "To Whom It May Concern," which feels impersonal and dated.

**You have a referral:** Lead with it immediately — "Marcus Webb on your platform team suggested I reach out about the Staff Engineer opening." Referrals are the most powerful opener available and should never be buried.

**You're underqualified:** Don't apologize. The skill will frame transferable skills assertively, highlight your learning velocity with concrete examples, and position your distance from the requirement as a near-gap rather than an absence.

**You're overqualified:** The skill adds a brief, confident explanation of why this role is the right move — returning to hands-on work, joining a mission-driven team, or focusing on a specific domain — so the concern is pre-empted before the reader forms it.

**Career change:** The skill bridges industries explicitly — naming the transferable skill, the common underlying challenge, and the proof point from your existing history that makes you credible in the new domain.

## Best Practices

- **One letter per application.** Batch-applying with the same letter is detectable and ineffective. The skill is fast enough that tailoring each one is practical.
- **Keep it under 400 words.** Hiring managers at high-volume companies spend an average of 30 seconds on a cover letter before deciding to read the resume. Respect their time.
- **Match the register of the company.** If the job posting uses informal language and emojis, a highly formal letter signals poor cultural read. If the posting is buttoned-up, a casual letter raises concerns.
- **Proofread the company name and role title.** This sounds obvious but is the single most common error — especially when using AI assistance to adapt letters quickly across multiple applications.
- **Send it even when it's "optional."** Optional cover letters are still read by managers who care about writing ability, communication, and cultural fit. Use the opening.
