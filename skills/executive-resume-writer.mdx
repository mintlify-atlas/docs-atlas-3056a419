Executive resumes are fundamentally different from individual contributor resumes. They lead with strategic impact, organizational scale, and business transformation — not technical skills or task completion. A hiring committee evaluating a CFO, CHRO, or VP of Engineering is not asking "what did you do?" — they are asking "what changed because you were there, and how big was that change?" The `executive-resume-writer` skill understands this distinction deeply. It restructures your career narrative around leadership brand, P&L ownership, and transformation story, and formats it in the two-to-three page standard that executive recruiters, boards, and PE firms expect.

## What Changes at the Executive Level

Executive resumes require a rethinking of format, emphasis, and voice — not just a longer version of a standard resume. Here is what shifts:

- **Format**: 2-page standard for VP/SVP; 2–3 pages acceptable for C-suite; longer CVs are appropriate for board presentations that require comprehensive career documentation
- **Executive summary replaces objective statement**: A 5–7 sentence narrative paragraph (not a bullet list) that defines your leadership brand, scope of experience, industries, and signature achievement — this is read before anything else
- **P&L ownership and budget scale go front and center**: Dollar amounts (revenue owned, budget managed, cost savings delivered) signal credibility to executive audiences more than any job title
- **Org size and management layers are explicit**: State both direct reports and total organizational footprint — "Led 12 direct reports across a 400-person global organization" is far more informative than "managed large teams"
- **Board exposure and investor relations**: Any board membership, board presentation responsibility, or investor relations work belongs prominently in the experience section and in its own Board & Advisory section
- **M&A experience, market entries, and major restructurings**: These are career-defining signals for senior executives — each should be named, scoped, and quantified (deal size, number of acquisitions, integration outcomes)
- **Leadership philosophy**: Unlike IC resumes, executive documents hint at *how* you lead — your operating style is as relevant as your track record to boards and C-suite hiring committees

## Executive Summary Formula

The executive summary is the single most important section of an executive resume. It replaces a generic objective statement with a leadership brand narrative. A strong executive summary covers all five elements:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Current Title and Scope">
    Open with your current or most recent function and the scale at which you operate. Use a declarative title header before the paragraph body (e.g., `CHIEF REVENUE OFFICER | B2B SAAS | GROWTH & GTM STRATEGY`).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Years of Relevant Experience and Industries">
    State the total years of executive-level experience and the industries where it was earned. Be specific: "18 years scaling B2B SaaS companies from Series B through IPO" is more compelling than "18 years in tech."
  </Step>
  <Step title="Key Functional Areas">
    Identify the 2–3 functional domains where your expertise is deepest — the areas you would be hired to lead. For a COO: operational excellence, M&A integration, and global team scaling. For a CMO: demand generation, brand strategy, and product marketing. These functional areas become the pillars of the rest of the document.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Organizational Scale Proof Point">
    Drop one concrete scale statement into the summary — headcount led, revenue owned, geographic footprint, or number of business units. This anchors your narrative in reality before the reader reaches your experience section.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Signature Achievement">
    Close the summary with your single most impressive career outcome, stated with a dollar figure or percentage if possible. This is the number that opens doors. Choose the one that most clearly demonstrates your value to the type of organization you're targeting next.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Example Executive Summary:**

```text
CHIEF OPERATING OFFICER | OPERATIONAL TRANSFORMATION | GLOBAL SCALE

Operations executive with 22 years building and scaling B2B technology businesses from
$30M to $600M in revenue. Deep expertise in operational transformation, M&A integration,
and global team development, with a track record spanning SaaS, enterprise software, and
infrastructure industries across North America and EMEA.

Led organizations of up to 800 employees across 6 countries, overseeing P&L for services
and professional services businesses with full budget authority of $180M. Architect of
3 successful exits and 2 IPO preparations, consistently delivering margin expansion
alongside top-line growth.

Known for building the operational infrastructure that lets companies scale without
breaking — and for developing the next generation of leaders who carry that infrastructure
forward after I move on.
```

## Key Executive Metrics

Executive resumes live and die by the credibility of their numbers. Every major bullet should be anchored to at least one of the following metric types:

| Metric Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Revenue owned or influenced | "$280M P&L" / "grew revenue from $50M to $210M" / "oversaw $1.2B business unit" |
| Organizational headcount | "12 direct reports, 650 total organization" / "scaled team from 80 to 400" |
| Cost savings or efficiency gains | "reduced COGS by $14M (18%)" / "cut infrastructure spend by 35% through vendor consolidation" |
| Market share growth | "grew market share from 8% to 19% in core segment over 3 years" |
| M&A activity | "led 4 acquisitions totaling $340M with 90% talent retention post-close" |
| Customer NPS or retention | "improved Net Promoter Score from 28 to 61 over 18 months" / "reduced churn from 14% to 7%" |
| Capital efficiency | "raised Series C ($75M) and Series D ($150M) while maintaining &lt;18-month runway burn" |
| Investor and board outcomes | "delivered 4.2x return for PE sponsor over 5-year hold" |

## Role-Specific Guidance

<Accordion title="CEO / President">
  Lead with total revenue and headcount under management. Emphasize vision setting, board dynamics, capital allocation, and company transformation narrative. Board relationships and investor outcomes are critical. Show progression from business unit leadership or divisional president to full P&L ownership.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="CFO">
  Open with the dollar scale of financial operations managed (revenue, balance sheet, or deal flow). Emphasize IPO preparation, audit committee interaction, M&A due diligence, and capital structure decisions. Include any debt raises, equity rounds, or divestitures with deal size.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="COO">
  Focus on operational transformation, margin improvement, and scalable infrastructure. "Before and after" framing is particularly powerful: inherited a 62% gross margin, exited at 79%. Show span of control (number of functions reporting in) and cross-functional integration wins.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="CHRO / CPO">
  Emphasize organizational design, talent strategy, and culture transformation at scale. Quantify people programs with business outcomes: "leadership development program reduced director-level attrition by 40%." DEI initiatives with measurable outcomes, succession planning, and compensation restructuring all belong here.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="CRO / CMO / VP Sales">
  Lead with quota attainment and revenue growth. Show the full go-to-market system you built: team size, territory design, sales cycle changes, and pipeline metrics. Marketing leaders should include CAC efficiency, pipeline contribution, and brand-to-revenue attribution.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="VP Engineering / CTO">
  Balance technical credibility with organizational leadership. Show headcount growth, engineering velocity improvements (deployment frequency, lead time), architectural transformation, and cost of infrastructure. Board-level technology communication is a differentiator for CTOs at growth-stage companies.
</Accordion>

## Executive Bullet Structure

Executive bullets use a different structure than IC bullets. Rather than the tech resume's `[action] + [what] + [impact]` formula, executive bullets follow a **transformation arc**:

```text
[Strategic context] → [Initiative you led] → [Business outcome at scale]
```

**Example — COO Transformation:**
```text
Inherited fragmented operations across 4 acquired businesses running separate ERP
systems and reporting structures; consolidated into unified operating model within
14 months, reducing overhead by $8M annually and enabling company's first
consolidated financial reporting for Series D due diligence.
```

**Example — CHRO People Strategy:**
```text
Partnered with CEO and board to design and implement a leadership development
pipeline for high-potential directors; program produced 11 internal VP promotions
over 3 years, reducing executive search spend by $2.4M and cutting time-to-
productivity for new VPs from 9 months to 4 months.
```

**Example — CRO Revenue Growth:**
```text
Rebuilt go-to-market motion for enterprise segment, shifting from field-only to
a hybrid inside/field model; hired and onboarded 45-person enterprise sales team,
expanded ACV from $28K to $95K, and grew enterprise ARR from $12M to $67M
over 30 months.
```

## Optimization Workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Define Your Leadership Brand">
    The skill begins by asking three questions: What type of executive are you? (Operator, Builder, Turnaround Artist, Scale Leader, etc.) What is the single most impressive number from your career? What do the people who've worked with you say makes you distinctive? The answers shape the summary and the narrative thread running through the document.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Construct the Executive Summary">
    Using your inputs, the skill drafts a 5–7 sentence executive summary paragraph that opens with your title-level header, establishes industry and functional depth, states organizational scale, and closes with your signature achievement. It avoids first-person pronouns and never reads as a list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build the Core Competencies Grid">
    Eight to twelve leadership competencies are selected and formatted as a visual grid. These are leadership themes — not skills. "Revenue Growth" is an executive competency. "Microsoft Excel" is not.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Write Role Entries with Executive Context">
    Each role entry opens with a 2–3 line context paragraph stating the company's scale (revenue, headcount, stage), your reporting structure, and your mandate. The bullets that follow are transformation-arc statements with dollar-scale metrics.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add Board, Advisory, and External Roles">
    Board memberships, advisory positions, investor roles, and significant speaking engagements or publications get their own section. These validate your external reputation and are particularly important for CEO, CFO, and board-level candidacies.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Format and Length Review">
    The skill checks that the resume lands at the right length for your level (2 pages for VP/SVP, 2–3 for C-suite), that typography and formatting support rapid scanning by a busy executive recruiter, and that the narrative arc from summary through experience is coherent and compelling.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  For VP and above, the summary section is read before anything else. Executive recruiters and board members make a preliminary judgment within the first eight seconds of reading — before they reach a single experience bullet. Lead with the biggest number or most impressive scope statement you have. If your signature achievement is a $400M exit, a 5x revenue growth story, or a 10,000-person org you built from scratch, that information belongs in sentence one or two of your summary, not buried in your third job entry.
</Note>

## Common Executive Resume Mistakes

- **Writing a longer IC resume instead of an executive resume** — length alone doesn't make a document executive-level; the frame, language, and emphasis must shift entirely
- **Listing responsibilities instead of transformations** — "Responsible for P&L of $200M division" is weaker than "Grew $200M division to $340M in 3 years through channel expansion into EMEA and APAC"
- **Omitting org scale context** — never mention team leadership without stating both direct reports and total organizational headcount
- **Burying the biggest number** — your career's most impressive quantified outcome belongs near the top of the document, not at the end of your oldest job entry
- **Using individual contributor action verbs** — "managed," "handled," and "assisted" signal middle management; use "orchestrated," "championed," "architected," "transformed," and "drove" instead
- **Leaving out board and advisory work** — even informal advisory roles demonstrate external credibility and peer validation that executive audiences value
