Resume bullets are necessarily brief — they have to be. A single line like "Redesigned checkout flow, reducing cart abandonment by 35%" is effective on a resume, but it hides the thinking, the tradeoffs, the collaboration, and the judgment that actually made the outcome possible. For designers, engineers, product managers, and consultants, portfolio case studies let you tell the full story of your best work: what was broken, what you chose to do about it, how you executed, and what you learned. This skill expands bullets into compelling project narratives that demonstrate not just what you accomplished, but how you think.

## Case Study Structure

A strong portfolio case study follows a six-part arc. Each section has a specific job — skip one and the story feels incomplete to the reader.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Problem Statement">
    Establish the stakes before describing any solution. What was broken, why did it matter, and who was affected? Quantify the problem if possible: "68% cart abandonment vs. a 55% industry average" lands harder than "high cart abandonment." Give enough context that a reader unfamiliar with your company understands why this work was worth doing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Constraints">
    Every real project operates under constraints. Name yours: timeline pressure (six weeks before holiday season), resource limits (two engineers, no designer budget), technical boundaries (no changes to payment integrations, must maintain PCI compliance). Constraints make your decisions legible — they explain why you chose one path over another and show that you operate in reality, not in ideal conditions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Approach">
    Describe your methodology and the key decisions you made along the way. What did you learn in research? What options did you consider? Why did you choose the direction you did over the alternatives? This section is where your thinking becomes visible — it's often the most differentiating part of a case study, because outcomes can be similar between candidates but reasoning processes rarely are.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Execution">
    Describe what you actually built or delivered, how the work came together, and how you collaborated with others. For designers, include visual artifacts — before/after comparisons, wireframe progressions, final screens. For engineers, describe architecture decisions, scale considerations, or technical tradeoffs. For PMs and consultants, describe the cross-functional coordination, the stakeholder alignment moments, and the pivots you made when reality diverged from the plan.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Results">
    Quantify outcomes and connect them to business impact wherever possible. A table showing before/after metrics is the clearest format. If you have secondary effects (customer support tickets dropped, return rate improved, engagement increased), include those — they show your work had systemic impact beyond the primary goal. Be honest about what you can and can't attribute to your specific work.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Lessons Learned">
    This section is what separates a case study from a brag sheet. Describe what you'd do differently — more A/B testing before a full launch, earlier stakeholder alignment, a different research methodology. Intellectual honesty here signals growth mindset and self-awareness, which hiring managers at senior levels weight heavily. It also makes the case study more credible: work that goes perfectly is work that wasn't hard enough.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What Inputs Are Needed

The richer the input you provide, the stronger the case study the skill can produce. At minimum, provide the following:

- **Resume bullet or project description** — the starting point; even a single sentence like "Led redesign of onboarding flow, improving activation by 22%" is enough to begin
- **Your specific role** — what you personally owned vs. what the team delivered collectively; the case study must be honest about your individual contribution
- **Team size and composition** — number of people, functions represented (design, engineering, data, marketing); shows scale of coordination
- **Timeline** — how long the project ran; helps the reader calibrate scope and pace
- **Metrics you have** — any before/after data, business impact numbers, or proxy metrics; if you don't have exact numbers, the skill can help you estimate or characterize impact directionally

<Note>
  The skill asks clarifying questions before drafting to extract maximum detail. The more context you share — what you debated, what failed, what surprised you — the stronger the case study. The first draft is a starting point for iteration, not a finished document.
</Note>

## Tailoring by Role

Different roles emphasize different dimensions of the case study. The skill adjusts its framing and emphasis based on the role type you specify:

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Designers">
    **Emphasize:** Process, user research, iteration, and the visual evolution of your work.

    Include wireframes, user testing insights, and the reasoning behind key design decisions. Show the messy middle — sketch explorations, options you rejected, and how user feedback changed your direction. The final polished screens matter less than demonstrating a rigorous, user-centered process.

    **Key signals:** User research methodology, usability testing results, design system contributions, iteration count before final, accessibility considerations.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Engineers">
    **Emphasize:** Technical decisions, architecture choices, scale, and the tradeoffs you navigated.

    Explain why you chose one technical approach over alternatives — the choice between consistency and availability, the decision to build vs. buy, the refactoring approach that enabled faster iteration. Quantify engineering outcomes: latency reduced, uptime improved, build time cut, error rate decreased.

    **Key signals:** System design decisions, scale handled, performance improvements, reliability engineering, code quality investments, cross-team API design.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Product Managers">
    **Emphasize:** Problem definition, prioritization, cross-functional coordination, and the link between your decisions and business outcomes.

    Show how you decided what to build and what not to build. Describe the stakeholder dynamics you navigated, the data that informed your prioritization, and how you aligned engineering, design, and business leadership on a shared direction.

    **Key signals:** How you defined the problem, your prioritization framework, what you said no to, how you measured success, the business outcome vs. the original goal.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Consultants">
    **Emphasize:** Client situation, diagnostic approach, recommendation quality, and measurable business outcome.

    Frame the case study around the client's problem and your firm's value add. Show how you structured the analysis, what hypothesis you tested, and how your recommendation changed what the client did. The outcome should be expressed in client business terms — revenue, cost, market share, risk reduction — not in consulting deliverable terms.

    **Key signals:** Problem scoping, stakeholder interviews, analytical framework, recommendation logic, client adoption of recommendation, measurable post-engagement outcome.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Case Study Formats by Context

Where the case study will live shapes how it should be structured:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Portfolio Website" icon="globe">
    Full six-section narrative with visual artifacts. Aim for a 5–10 minute read time. Optimize for skimmability: use headers, pull quotes, and a metrics summary box at the top so fast readers get the key facts without reading every word.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Interview Leave-Behind" icon="file-text">
    A condensed one-to-two page PDF. Lead with the headline result, then summarize problem, approach, and outcome. Drop the Lessons Learned section for printed formats — save that for the verbal discussion in the room.
  </Card>
  <Card title="LinkedIn Featured Section" icon="linkedin">
    A post or article format, 800–1,200 words. Open with the headline result, tell the story conversationally, end with a reflection on what you'd do differently. Use LinkedIn native formatting — headers, bullets, and line breaks — for mobile readability.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Application Work Sample" icon="briefcase">
    Requested by the hiring team for a specific role. Tailor the emphasis to the role's requirements — if they're hiring for your problem-framing ability, expand the Problem Statement and Approach sections. If they're hiring for execution speed, emphasize the timeline and delivery cadence.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Results Section: Getting the Numbers Right

The Results section is often the hardest to write because candidates either over-claim (taking credit for outcomes shaped by many factors) or under-claim (hedging so heavily the impact disappears). The skill helps you find the right calibration:

**Use a before/after table** when you have clean comparative data:

```markdown
| Metric              | Before   | After    | Change   |
|---------------------|----------|----------|----------|
| Cart abandonment    | 68%      | 44%      | -35%     |
| Mobile conversion   | 18%      | 41%      | +128%    |
| Revenue per visitor | $2.40    | $3.85    | +60%     |
```

**Use directional language when exact numbers aren't available:**
- "Reduced mean time to resolution from hours to minutes" (directional, credible)
- "Increased feature adoption meaningfully, as measured by 30-day retention of new users" (metric exists, number is internal)

**Be explicit about attribution:**
- "Our team's work contributed to a 22% improvement in activation, measured over the 90 days following launch" is more credible than "I improved activation by 22%"

## Best Practices

- **Write for an audience who doesn't know your company.** Never assume the reader knows your product, your team, or the industry context. Define acronyms, explain the business, and establish why the problem mattered before describing your solution.
- **Show your work, not just your conclusions.** Readers can verify outcomes; they want to understand the reasoning. A case study that shows you considered three approaches and explains why you chose one signals better judgment than one that presents only the chosen path.
- **Keep confidential information out.** If you're describing client work or proprietary systems, anonymize company names, redact specific financial figures where required, and check whether your employment contract or NDA permits portfolio use of the work. When in doubt, describe the problem domain and your process without naming the client.
- **Make your individual contribution explicit.** In collaborative projects, be specific about what you personally owned — "I led the user research and synthesized findings into the product brief; the engineering team built the implementation" is honest and still compelling.
- **Update case studies after the fact.** If a project's downstream impact becomes clearer months after launch, add a postscript to the case study with the updated numbers. Longitudinal results ("six months post-launch, retention had improved a further 8%") are powerful signals that you track outcomes over time.
