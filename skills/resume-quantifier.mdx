Numbers make resume bullets significantly more likely to catch a recruiter's eye — and more importantly, they make achievements credible and comparable. "Improved customer satisfaction" could mean anything. "Improved CSAT from 72% to 91% over two quarters" tells a hiring manager exactly what you accomplished and at what scale. The `resume-quantifier` skill scans every bullet on your resume for untapped quantification opportunities, guides you through a discovery process to surface real metrics from memory, and applies structured estimation frameworks when exact figures aren't available. The goal isn't to impress with big numbers — it's to make every claim specific enough to be believed.

## Types of Metrics

Every professional role generates measurable evidence. The skill looks for all of the following categories across your experience:

**Scale** — how big was the scope of your work?
- Team size managed or mentored
- Number of users, customers, or accounts served
- Revenue or budget owned or influenced
- Number of projects, products, or initiatives managed simultaneously

**Improvement** — what got better because of you?
- Percentage increase in revenue, engagement, conversion, or retention
- Percentage decrease in cost, error rate, churn, or cycle time
- Before/after quality scores (CSAT, NPS, uptime percentage)
- Efficiency gains expressed as time or resource savings

**Speed** — how fast did things happen?
- Time to completion vs. baseline or industry standard
- Cycle time reduction (from X days to Y days)
- Response time improvements (from X hours to Y hours)
- Time saved per week, month, or year through process changes

**Volume** — how much output did you produce?
- Tickets resolved, calls handled, or customers onboarded per day/week
- Features shipped, campaigns launched, or reports delivered per quarter
- Transactions processed, records managed, or documents reviewed annually

**Financial** — what was the dollar impact?
- Revenue generated or influenced
- Cost savings or budget reductions delivered
- ROI of initiatives you led
- Deal sizes closed or contracts negotiated

## Estimation Frameworks

When you don't have exact figures, the skill uses four structured approaches to arrive at conservative, defensible estimates. These are not guesses — they're reasoned approximations you can explain and defend in an interview.

**Industry benchmarks** — the skill references typical ranges for your role and seniority level. A mid-level sales rep at a SaaS company closing deals in the $50K–$150K range has a defensible basis for revenue claims even without exact CRM exports.

**Input-to-output calculation** — work backwards from what you know. If you met with 5 clients per week for 50 working weeks, that's 250+ client engagements. If each took 30 minutes of prep, that's 125+ hours of client work annually. Inputs you can count become outputs you can cite.

**Relative comparisons** — when absolute numbers are sensitive or unavailable, relative markers are credible: "ranked in the top 10% of the sales team," "first on the team to complete the certification," "outperformed department average by 15%."

**Conservative rounding** — always estimate down. If you think the improvement was about 60%, the bullet says "~50%." If your team was 10–14 people depending on the quarter, the bullet says "8–12." Recruiters and interviewers respond better to modest claims you can back up than to impressive figures that fall apart under questioning.

## The Discovery Process

When you share a bullet with no metrics, the skill runs a structured discovery sequence — not generic questions, but targeted prompts based on your role type:

**For sales roles:** What was your quota? What percentage did you hit? How many deals did you close? What was the average deal size? How did you rank on the team?

**For marketing roles:** What were the follower, traffic, or lead numbers at the start and end of your tenure? What was the campaign budget? What was the cost per lead or conversion rate?

**For customer-facing roles:** How many accounts or tickets did you handle? What were the satisfaction scores? What was the response time target and did you beat it?

**For technical roles:** What were the performance numbers before and after your changes? What was the system's uptime? How many users were affected by the improvements you shipped?

**For management roles:** How many direct reports? What was the team's output before and after you joined? Did retention, engagement, or performance improve on your watch?

<Note>
  Always confirm estimated metrics against your own memory before adding them to your resume. Approximate numbers you can confidently explain in an interview — "my best estimate based on the ticket queue we saw every day" — are far better than precise-sounding figures you can't back up. If you genuinely can't recall even a rough number, use relative language ("top quartile," "above team average") rather than fabricating specifics.
</Note>

## Common "I Have No Numbers" Scenarios

These are the most frequent situations where people think quantification isn't possible — and the workaround the skill uses:

**"I was just one person on a large team"** — focus on your individual contribution. "Contributed 3 of the 12 features shipped in Q3" or "Wrote 40% of the front-end code for the redesign" shows meaningful scope without overstating your role.

**"I don't have access to business metrics"** — quantify your activities and inputs instead. "Created 50+ sales presentations," "Managed pipeline of 200+ prospects in Salesforce," and "Produced 75-page technical documentation" are all credible even without access to outcome data.

**"My results were confidential"** — use percentage-only or range formats. "$X–$Y million revenue contribution" or "40%+ year-over-year growth" conveys scale without revealing proprietary figures.

**"My role didn't produce measurable outcomes"** — measure the work itself: accuracy rate, throughput, speed, or reliability. "Processed 200+ records daily with 99.5% accuracy, exceeding team average by 15%" turns a data-entry role into a credible achievement.

**"I was entry-level with no real impact"** — quantify learning velocity, accuracy, onboarding speed, and throughput. "Completed 3-week onboarding in 9 days and independently managed a customer queue within 2 weeks" shows initiative and aptitude even without business-level metrics.

## Quantification Quality Checklist

Before finalizing each bullet, the skill verifies:

- ✅ At least one number is present
- ✅ The number is relevant — it adds meaning, not noise
- ✅ The scale is clear (what does the number represent?)
- ✅ Any estimate is conservative and explainable
- ✅ The number adds credibility without requiring a footnote
- ✅ You can explain how you know this figure in an interview
- ✅ No more than 2–3 metrics per bullet (more becomes cluttered)

## Numbers to Avoid

Not every number makes a bullet stronger. The skill also flags metric choices that can backfire:

- ❌ Numbers that inadvertently reveal confidential company data
- ❌ Figures that look impressive on paper but collapse under interview questioning
- ❌ Percentages without a baseline ("increased by 300%" — from 1 to 3?)
- ❌ Numbers so small they invite comparison ("team of 2")
- ❌ Exact figures you invented rather than estimated from memory
- ❌ Metrics that don't connect to the work described in the same bullet
